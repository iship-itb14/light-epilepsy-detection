What a small model could mean for the person in that moment.

What we found, what it could mean for people living with epilepsy, and what it does not yet show.

We began with a simple question: could the warning that a seizure is starting be made small enough to wear? Most of the fifty million people with epilepsy live far from a hospital, and many live far from any specialist. For their families, the hardest part is often not the seizure itself but not knowing when it will come, or whether anyone will notice in time. A doctor can recognise a seizure by reading the brain's electrical signal, but nobody can watch that signal all day and all night. We wanted to know whether a computer program could do some of that watching. We also wanted it to be modest enough to run on a cheap, battery-powered device rather than a powerful computer far away. To find out, we taught two programs to recognise seizures in hospital recordings from fourteen children. Then we tested each program on a child it had never seen before, one child at a time. That last step matters, because a warning device will always meet a stranger, never someone it has already studied.

The small program did almost as well as one nearly two hundred times its size. Across all fourteen children, it caught 80 of 128 seizures, while the larger program caught 83. For a typical child, the small program caught more than nine seizures in every ten. It also raised a little more than one false alarm an hour, slightly fewer than the larger program did. In plain terms, size did not buy much: the extra bulk of the bigger program added almost nothing a family would notice. That is the central finding of this project. It suggests that the skill needed to spot many seizures does not require a large, expensive machine. It can be packed into something very small.

We then asked what a wearable could realistically carry. A hospital cap uses many sensors spread across the head, which no one would wear at home. We tried again with only two sensors, one just above each ear, where a headband or an earpiece could sit. Remarkably, the programs caught about as many seizures with two sensors as with the full cap. We also stored the small program in a more compact form, the way a photo can be saved at a smaller size. The complete detector then took up less than ten kilobytes, less space than a single photo on a phone. None of these savings cost a measurable number of seizures. Together, they mean the idea could, in principle, fit on the kind of cheap chip found in everyday gadgets.

These results also have clear limits, and we want to be plain about them. For three of the fourteen children, neither program caught a single seizure. Some of their seizures lasted only a few seconds, and others looked to the programs just like ordinary brain activity. One false alarm an hour is still too many for daily life; a parent woken that often would soon stop trusting the alarm. All the recordings came from one children's hospital, so we do not yet know how the programs would do for adults or with different equipment. We also showed how easily a result like ours can be made to look better than it is. If recordings are carelessly mixed before testing, the same program appears to catch noticeably more seizures than it really does. That is why we tested only on children each program had never met, and why we report the misses as openly as the successes. This is a research study, not a medical device, and nothing here has been tested in clinical care.

Even so, the story ends on a hopeful note. A warning device does not need to be large, costly or connected to a distant computer to be useful. Our smallest detector, listening to just two sensors, found most seizures in children it had never met. The next steps are to test it on adults from another hospital, to try it on a real wearable chip, and to let it adjust to each person during a short first week of use. Each of those steps could make it more reliable and better suited to everyday life. If that work succeeds, a family far from any specialist might one day have a quiet companion that notices when something is wrong. It would not replace a doctor, and it would not catch every seizure. But it could mean that fewer seizures happen with no one there to help.
