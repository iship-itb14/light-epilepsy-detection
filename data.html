Analysis · Part one

From hospital recordings to a fair test

Before a network can learn what a seizure looks like, days of continuous brain recordings have to become something it can read. This page shows every step we took, why we took it, and how to repeat it.

Each section is written for anyone. The settings, code and files behind it sit in a panel beside it.

The route

Eight steps, four of them done

The project moves left to right. The top brackets show which page of this site covers each step. The branches are the extra tests that check whether a result can be trusted.

    01 Gather · the nature of the data

    Hours of sleep, play and seizures, recorded at a children's hospital

    Our main recordings come from Boston Children's Hospital, where children with hard-to-treat epilepsy were monitored for days at a time after their medication was paused. Small metal discs on the scalp picked up the brain's electrical activity, the signal called EEG, without a break.

    Specialists then went through every recording and marked the second each seizure began and ended. Those marks are what the network learns from. We chose the 14 children who had at least five seizures and the same electrode layout in every file, which gives about 480 hours of signal and 128 seizures.

    02 What a seizure looks like in the data
    
    
    Forty seconds that look different from everything around them
    
    
    
      
    Below are 88 real seconds from one child, six of the eighteen channels. For the first stretch the lines wander quietly. Then, at the moment the doctors marked, the rhythm sharpens and grows across the whole head, and forty seconds later it gives way to slower waves.
    
      
    The network never sees this picture. It sees the numbers underneath it, four seconds at a time, and must decide whether each slice belongs to the shaded part.

    03 Clean
    
    
    Keep the brain, lose the hum
    
    
    
      
    A hospital room is electrically noisy. The building's power lines hum at 60 cycles a second, and slow drifts creep in when a child sweats or shifts. Neither tells us anything about seizures.
    
      
    So we keep only the range where brain rhythms live, between half a cycle and forty cycles a second, and discard the rest. The chart shows the result on ten minutes of one channel: everything right of the line is gone, including the sharp spike from the power supply.
    
      
    Windows with a dead electrode or a huge jolt from movement are marked, not thrown away. That keeps every choice reversible. Seizure windows are never removed, because a violent seizure can look like a jolt.

    04 Cut and label
    
    
    Four-second slices, and the honest problem with them
    
    
    
      
    Each recording is cut into slices of four seconds, short enough for a small device to judge quickly, long enough to hold a few brain rhythms. A slice is called a seizure if at least half of it falls inside the doctors' marks.
    
      
    That leaves 435,067 slices, and only 1,838 of them are seizures: about one in every 236. A model that always answered "no seizure" would be right 99.6% of the time and useless. This is why the results page will not lead with accuracy.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        04Labelling rule4 s · no overlap · label = overlap ≥ 0.5 · +2 s shifted copies (train only)+
        
    
          
    
          
    The exact overlap fraction is stored for every window, so the 0.5 rule can change later without cleaning again. 1,851 extra seizure windows, shifted by 2 s, are kept for training only.

      05 Split by patient
      
    
      No child is in both the lesson and the exam
      
    
      
      
      Every person's brain signal has its own signature. If slices from one child appear in both training and testing, a model can score well by recognising the child rather than the seizure. That would tell us nothing about a new patient, who is the only person who matters.
      
      
      So each child sits entirely on one side. Nine teach the model, two help us tune it, and three are kept sealed until the end. For the final numbers we repeat the whole process fourteen times, leaving out a different child each time.

      06 What we will ask the models
      
    
      One question, every four seconds: is this a seizure?
      
    
      
      
      Each model reads one slice, all eighteen channels at once, and gives a number between 0 and 1. Above a threshold, the slice counts as a warning. To keep false alarms down, an alarm sounds only when two of three slices in a row agree.
      
      
      We compare two networks. The first is a conventional one, large enough to be a fair yardstick. The second was designed for brain signals and is about a hundred times smaller. Then we shrink it further, and measure how much detection survives.
