Research · The full study

What we found, and how far to trust it

Four questions, each tested on children the model had never seen: can a tiny network find seizures, how much does a careless split flatter a result, can a wearable get by with two electrodes, and can the model shrink to fit one.

Each section is written for anyone. The settings, files and exact numbers behind it sit in a panel beside it.

01 The honest test · leave one child out

Every child is tested by a model that never met them

We trained each model 14 times. Each time, one child was set aside completely, the model learned from ten others, three more were used to set its alarm level, and only then was it tested on the child it had never seen. Every number on this page comes from those 14 unseen children.

The small network, EEGNet, has 1,729 weights. The baseline network has 328,385, about 190 times more. The small one caught 80 of the 128 seizures, the large one 83. For the typical child, the small one also raised fewer false alarms: a little more than one an hour.

The average hides a wide spread. Six children had every seizure, or all but one, caught by both models. Three children had none caught by either. Their seizures are either very short (a median of 8 seconds for chb16) or look no different from their normal EEG to these models (chb06).

02 The shuffle trap · why the split matters

A shuffled split adds about seven points

Many tutorials use a popular seizure table in which every recording has already been cut into one-second pieces. If those pieces are shuffled before the data is split, a test piece almost always has neighbours from the same recording in training. The model can then recognise the recording rather than the seizure.

We trained the same small network twice on the original recordings behind that table, changing only the split. With shuffled pieces it caught 95.8% of seizure pieces. With whole recordings kept apart it caught 88.7%. Accuracy barely moved, from 98.8% to 97.4%, because most pieces are not seizures. That is why we never report accuracy on its own.

The split is not the whole story. Even kept apart, these short, clean, hand-picked clips score far higher than long hospital recordings do. The near-perfect numbers seen online come mostly from the data, with the shuffle adding on top.

03 Fewer electrodes · fit for a wearable

Two channels do about as well as eighteen

A hospital cap has 18 channels. Nobody will wear that at home. Before seeing any results, we picked two smaller sets near the ears, where a headband or ear-worn device could sit: four channels, and just two, one on each side.

Both models were retrained from scratch on each set, with everything else unchanged. Seizures caught stayed between 80 and 88 of 128 in every case. With one training run each, differences of a few seizures are within chance, so we read this as "about the same", not "better".

Some children did lose out. One child's seizures were almost all missed with two channels, likely because they start away from the ears. Fewer channels save electrodes, data and battery, but barely shrink the model itself.

04 Smaller numbers · 8-bit compression

Eight-bit numbers, the same seizures caught

Models normally store each weight as a 32-bit number. Small chips prefer 8-bit numbers, which take a quarter of the space. We converted all 84 trained models to 8-bit without retraining them, and tested them on the same unseen children.

Detection held. Every version stayed within three seizures of its original, which is less than the variation we expect from training alone. The baseline network became 3.7 times smaller, from about 1.3 MB to 350 KB. The two-channel EEGNet became a complete detector in 9.7 KB, about 36 times smaller than the compressed baseline, catching 82 seizures against its 85.

05 Fewer weights · pruning Results due 29 Nov 2026

Pruning: running now

Pruning switches off the weights that matter least, then lets the model relearn for a while with them gone. We are pruning the two-channel models to 50, 70 and 80% zeros and testing each on the same unseen children.

06 Limits · what this does not show

What these results do not show

We wrote our list of weak spots before the results came in, so we could not explain them away afterwards. These are the ones that matter most for reading this page.

  1. Children from one hospital.All recordings come from one children's hospital in Boston. We have not yet tested adults or other equipment. A test on adult recordings from a hospital in Siena, Italy, without retraining, is planned next.
  2. One training run per setting.Each result comes from a single random seed. Differences of a few seizures may be chance.
  3. Three children are never caught.chb06, chb14 and chb16 have no seizures caught by any model, at any channel count.
  4. Scaling looks ahead.Each recording is scaled using its whole hour, including the future. A wearable would need a running estimate.
  5. False alarms are counted strictly.We give no grace period around seizures, so our rate is not directly comparable with work that does.
  6. Hospital signal, not a headband.Two channels from a hospital cap are cleaner than a device worn while moving.
  7. Speed measured on a laptop.Times compare formats with each other. We did not run the models on a real wearable chip.
  8. Not a medical device.Nothing here has been clinically tested.
